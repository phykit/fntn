THE CORPUS NOW STORES TEXT, AND THAT IS WHAT CLOSED THE THREE
  API, BlackBerry and Opera were diagnosed on 27 August as a corpus-fetch defect
  belonging to §13 row 22 rather than a fence defect: issuer names sitting in
  Cornell LII's page furniture, on thirteen documents that name no company at
  all. The first repair stripped the furniture, removing <nav>, <header> and
  <footer> and any element whose class or id contains nav, menu, sidebar,
  related, footer or breadcrumb. It removed 154,132 of 638,883 bytes, 24% of the
  corpus, and it removed none of the three. The fence still refused API 13
  times, BlackBerry 13 times and Opera 13 times, once per document.

  The reason is that none of the three was in the furniture the rule names.
    API         an HTML comment in <head>: <!-- API url -->, labelling the
                script that sets LII's search endpoint.
    BlackBerry  string literals inside an inline <script> in <head>, in a
    Opera       user-agent sniffer: /BlackBerry/i, /Opera Mini/i.
  <head> is not <header>, a comment carries no class or id, and a <script> block
  carries none either. The strip was doing what it was specified to do; the
  specification did not reach these three.

  Naming <script>, <style> and comments as three further things to remove would
  have closed those three and left the next member of the class to be discovered
  by the fence again, which is the same repair a fourth time. The corpus
  therefore stores EXTRACTED TEXT: <script> and <style> subtrees dropped entire,
  comments dropped, the furniture rules still applied, and the text that
  survives written to disk. A construct that carries no text cannot put a name
  in the corpus, so the class is closed rather than three of its members.

  Removed: 542,878 of 638,883 bytes, 85% of the corpus, against 24% before.
  Fence hits on the corpus: 0 distinct, 0 total, against 3 and 39 before.
  Files are now .txt rather than .htm, which is the warning to the next reader
  that markup is gone and with it a table's structure and a link's target.

  WHAT WAS FETCHED, AND WHEN. An earlier issue of this report said both that
  the documents were not re-fetched and that all thirteen were fetched again.
  Both describe something that happened and the two together describe nothing,
  so here is the sequence.

  THE NETWORK WAS HIT. Thirteen GETs to law.cornell.edu on 27 August 2026,
  08:07 UTC, one per document. That is the fetch of record for the raw pages.

  The corpus text in the tree was NOT produced by those GETs. It was produced
  by running the fetch script's extractor over the chrome-stripped HTML already
  committed, on the argument that element removal commutes with text
  extraction. The 27 August GETs were the check on that argument: the pages
  were put through the pipeline whole and compared, every raw size matched the
  26 August manifest to the byte, and every extracted document came out
  byte-identical to the one produced from the tree. The pages had not changed
  between the two fetches.

  THE RAW PAGES ARE NOW KEPT, at corpora/us/_raw, underscore-prefixed so both
  the corpus reader and the integrity check skip them, with their digests in
  corpora/us/_raw/_fetch.tsv. Extraction is destructive, and until this the
  corpus could not be re-derived from anything: raw_bytes was a number with
  nothing behind it and a change to the extractor could be tested only against
  itself. test_raw_html_reextracts_to_the_stored_corpus now re-derives all
  thirteen through the script's own extractor and compares byte-for-byte.

  THE HONEST LIMIT. What is kept is the 27 AUGUST fetch, not the 26 August one.
  The 26 August raw pages were overwritten by the chrome strip at fetch time
  and are gone. The two fetches agree on every byte count and on every
  extracted document, which is strong evidence they were the same pages, and it
  is evidence rather than the pages themselves. The manifest's retrieved_at
  still reads 26 August, because that is when the document in the corpus was
  first retrieved; _raw/_fetch.tsv reads 27 August, because that is when the
  page beside it was.

  THE INTEGRITY FLOOR MOVED, from 4,000 bytes to 500. The standard has not
  relaxed; the quantity has changed, a document as text being between a
  twentieth and a quarter of its own markup. 4,000 applied to text would refuse
  three of these thirteen, including Rule 16a-13, which is one sentence long,
  complete, and 763 bytes. The floor is derived from both populations rather
  than chosen: the smallest genuine document extracts to 763 bytes, and the two
  non-documents to hand extract to 54 (LII's 404 page) and 377 (the sec.gov
  stub this script already refuses). 500 is the round number in the gap. That
  gap is a factor of two and is stated rather than smoothed. The byte floor is
  no longer the main guard against a document that is not there: the extractor
  now refuses an unclosed dropped element directly, which is the failure the old
  four-fifths ratio test existed to catch and which no ratio can express once
  the output is text.

  Corpus integrity after the change: 13 documents, smallest 763 bytes against a
  500-byte floor, no undersized and no duplicated documents, and the manifest
  agreeing with the disk on every row.

  The ticker rule's own false negative is unchanged and is restated here because
  nothing above touches it: a bare ticker in lower or title case is invisible to
  the fence. That cost was taken over the alternative, a common-word filter on
  the ticker set, which would have made at least 95 real US issuers invisible in
  all capitals as well and would still have left CFR, LII, III and VII needing a
  lexicon row.
